# Goals

The planner's charter: what every change and every place in the queue serves, in order of priority, and the rubric that places each item. Every queue decision in `docs/queue.md` cites its tier and the data behind it: a benchmark, a CI time, a gate result, a token count, a wall time, a finding, or the user's request.

1. UI: a pleasant UI and UX first, in carat's Facet design language (calm, readable, carat's own personality, never a copy of another editor). The user's now-request of 2026-11-10 ("I want now a pleasant UI and UX as priority"): its UI items are T2, ahead of every T3 to T6 item and of the pipeline items, only T0 before them. Earlier, at `cursor-shapes`, the user put the UI first in this order: the buffer list; the bars (a global status bar with the mode pill and the reserved `lsp` slot, buffer tabs); the leader layout; floating windows; then, after code intelligence, the rest of the UI track, `markdown-preview`, `csv-viewer`, and `indent-guides`.
2. Code intelligence, second at the user's request: the language server client with diagnostics filling the bar's `lsp` slot; completion as you type (fuzzy matching, kind icons, the type on the right, Enter inserts and Tab replaces); the highlighted item's documentation beside the list, fetched lazily; K quick documentation in a scrollable popup with markdown rendered and a key to pin it into a split; parameter info; go to and peek definition; buffer-word completion with Ctrl-N and Ctrl-P. Requests and documentation text from a language server are untrusted: escape sequences are stripped before the terminal, requests are asynchronous and cancelled when stale, and a key pays no cost for them.
3. Security, never traded for the goals above: no merge without the security review of the committed diff and its result in the pull request, never skipped; a High or Medium finding at confidence 8 or above blocks the merge, and blocking security fixes go to the front of the queue (T0) whatever the order of the goals. At the end of each sprint of five merged changes, `sprint-planning` reviews the sprint's combined diff, and the whole codebase every second sprint; a delivery never runs that review itself.
4. Token and pipeline efficiency: a change delivered in few tokens and little wall time, CI kept fast, by a pipeline that never forgets and improves itself (the retrospective may also simplify the workflow or improve its script), with agents effective at writing code. The user made this goal 1 in `pipeline-speed`, when a change took about an hour and 1.1M to 1.7M total tokens, and moved it below the UI and code intelligence at `cursor-shapes`: pipeline items yield to features (the rubric).
5. Performance at run time, kept by the benchmark and a history of benchmark results.
6. Code quality the Ruby way, within Spinel's rules.
7. Clean Architecture that is easy to extend.
8. Code simple and elegant for the people who read it.
9. Neovim command support.
10. The README kept current, including how carat is built with AI.
11. Documentation size and quality controlled.

## The rubric

Every item of `docs/queue.md` names its tier and its size, as `(T2 user request, estimated small)`, and every queue decision cites its tier and a number; `make docs-lint` refuses an item without both.

- Tiers, in order: T0 a blocking High or Medium security finding at confidence 8 or above, a red `main`, or a crash; T1 pipeline defects and pipeline efficiency (tokens, wall time, rework, CI time); T2 user-reported bugs and now-requests; T3 Low security findings reachable from an untrusted project without extra user action; T4 other efficiency (run time, tests), ordered by cost of delay; T5 roadmap items and user features, in the order of the goals above; T6 other Lows and advisories, folded into a change of the same domain and queued alone only when five accumulate.
- Inside T3 to T6, order by value divided by size. A quick win may jump ahead within T4 to T6, never ahead of T0 to T3. Same-domain quick wins are batched into one change to share its fixed overhead, 1.1M to 1.7M total tokens a change before `pipeline-speed`.
- Sizes are total-token budgets, input plus output as the workflow's usage reports them, estimated from `docs/metrics.tsv`: small under 500k and 25 minutes, medium under 1.2M, large under 2.5M. The history records each change's estimate beside its total and output tokens and its wall minutes, and the retrospective flags an overrun (`sh tools/run_state.sh budget CHANGE`).
- Size check before a change starts: a brief with more than about four separate fixes, or estimated large outside the roadmap, is split into balanced changes. `workflow-trim` bundled seven fixes and needed four quality rounds across two runs; `guard-allowlist`, three fixes with four folds, took 5 rework rounds and 860,176 output tokens.
- The planner runs when the queue needs a decision: a new finding, a user request, or a split; otherwise the next item is taken as written.
- Aging: an item records how often it was pushed back; at three it rises one tier or gets a written reason.
- Sprints (the user's decision of 2026-11-11, replacing the rule of at most one non-feature change per three features): the queue is a sprint of 5 changes, at most one of them a pipeline change (the workflow, agents, CI, tools, process docs); T0 items are exempt and do not count. Each sprint item is self-contained: its locations, why with its numbers, a fix outline, the acceptance check, and the headings of the backlog entries it covers, since no delivery opens the backlog; the rest of the queue is the pool the next sprint draws from, features first. A feature is a now-request of the user, a feature the user requested or scheduled, or a user-visible roadmap feature the user approved, such as `search-and-substitute`. A feature passes T1 to T6 items, which otherwise keep their order among themselves; it never passes a T0 item nor the track it depends on (code intelligence after floating windows; `markdown-preview` and `csv-viewer` after the UI track); when no feature is ready, a plan decision says so and the next non-feature item runs.
- Pace, price, and quality (the user's decision of 2026-11-11): about one merged change per hour of running time, reported per sprint in `docs/sprints.tsv`; budgets are tracked, never enforced, an overrun reported in the run's report and the planner's decisions but no run stopped for it; quality held: the security review never skipped, check and CI green, blocking findings fixed in the run, advisories recorded and never chased mid-run nor re-raised as blocking in a later round.
- A brief part, plan note, or proposal states the problem, its numbers, and the acceptance check, never the implementation, which the contributor chooses. In `workflow-trim` a blocking quality finding reversed the approach a brief part, a retrospective note, and a plan note had all prescribed.

## Calibration

`CALIBRATION` in `.claude/workflows/deliver-change.js` sets each kind of agent start's model tier (`cheap`, the agent file's `model:` line, or `session`) and reasoning effort (`low` or `session`), and the history's `calibration` column records what each run used, beside its phase tokens and calls and its first passes.

- Reasons: mechanical starts (resume, the scout plan, record, checker, benchmark, merger) run at low effort, the four with an agent file on the cheap tier, the checker passing first 21 times in the 22 rows before `agent-calibration`; judgment starts (planner, contributor, quality, security, close) keep the session's model and effort, the quality review passing first only 13 times in 35.
- First move, at `agent-calibration`: the scouts from the session's effort to low, the largest mechanical cost left at a default (over 33,000 output tokens on 18 of those 22 rows, up to 116,958; plan plus scout 26 to 37 percent of a run's output on many). The record start's cheap tier waits for an agent file to name it.
- Rule: one kind moves at a time. A move is kept when, over the next five rows with it, the gates' first-pass rates and the rework rounds are no worse than over the five rows before it; otherwise the planner queues its revert. The security reviewer never moves to a cheaper tier or a lower effort.
